# Propofol: From General Anesthesia to Migraine Disorder

## One-Sentence Summary

Propofol is an intravenous anesthetic and sedative-hypnotic. The data compiled for this report do not include a US-approved indication text for it.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **migraine disorder**,
with **3 clinical trials** and **20 publications** currently supporting this direction, including several randomized controlled trials in the emergency department.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism-of-action data are not available in the compiled data. Based on the mechanistic rationale in the data, propofol is a GABA-A positive allosteric modulator. Enhanced GABAergic inhibition may dampen trigeminovascular pain signaling and cortical hyperexcitability.

In preclinical work, propofol hemisuccinate suppressed cortical spreading depression (PMID 22390898). This phenomenon is believed to underlie migraine aura and may also trigger migraine pain. This offers a plausible biological link between a sedative-hypnotic and migraine.

In practice, low (sub-anesthetic) doses have been used for refractory migraine in the emergency department. Because of the risks of sedation and respiratory depression, use is limited to monitored settings.

## Safety Considerations

- **Disease-level contraindication signals (knowledge graph, not label text)**: epilepsy, hypotensive disorder, respiratory failure, intracranial arteriosclerosis, inherited lipid metabolism disorder and familial hyperlipidemia.
- **Clinical setting**: sedation and respiratory depression risk limit use to monitored settings such as the emergency department.
- **Drug Interactions**: 100 interactions were identified. Most listed are moderate, such as famotidine, clarithromycin, levofloxacin, ondansetron, granisetron, dolasetron, cisapride, morphine and opium, as well as several laxatives (bisacodyl, lactulose, magnesium citrate). Metronidazole is minor.

Please refer to the package insert for the full warnings and contraindications, which were not available in the compiled data.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Proceed with Guardrails**

**Rationale:**
Several randomized trials and systematic reviews support low-dose propofol for acute migraine in the emergency department, including pediatric populations. However, the trials are small, one was terminated early, and the evidence is mostly limited to monitored settings. All US licenses are currently cancelled, and label safety data are missing.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Obtain and review the FDA package insert warnings and contraindications (currently a blocking gap)
- Confirm the mechanism of action from DrugBank
- Appraise the RCT results (efficacy, relapse and adverse events) in a formal evidence synthesis
- Define a monitored-use protocol (dosing, respiratory and hemodynamic monitoring, exclusion of patients with respiratory failure, hypotension or epilepsy)
- Clarify the regulatory and supply status given the cancelled US licenses
