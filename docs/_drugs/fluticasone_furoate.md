# Fluticasone Furoate: From Its Marketed Use to Atopic Eczema

## One-Sentence Summary

Fluticasone furoate is a corticosteroid marketed in the US as an inhaled powder and a nasal spray. The original approved indication is not recorded in the source data.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **atopic eczema**, with **12 clinical trials** and **2 publications** retrieved. Almost all of the trials test fluticasone *propionate* (a different salt), so the support is indirect and class-level.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Currently, detailed mechanism of action data is not available. Based on known information, fluticasone furoate is a high-potency glucocorticoid receptor agonist. Glucocorticoids reduce Th2 cytokines and limit eosinophil and T-cell infiltration in tissue. This is the same inflammatory pathway that drives atopic eczema, so the class-level rationale is plausible.

Topical corticosteroids are already standard care for atopic eczema. The trials retrieved show this: fluticasone propionate cream is used as the active treatment or comparator in several atopic dermatitis studies. However, these trials do not test the furoate salt. The TxGNN score is a model prediction, not clinical evidence.

There is also a route issue. The marketed furoate products in the data are an inhaled powder (Ellipta) and a nasal spray. No dermatologic (topical) furoate product appears, so the route needed for atopic eczema is not confirmed as compatible.

## Safety Considerations

Please refer to the package insert for safety information. The source data has no warnings or contraindications, and the drug-interaction query returned no results.

Related literature (a review of intranasal corticosteroids) and a pediatric HPA-axis trial of topical fluticasone propionate (NCT00546000) both point to systemic corticosteroid effects as a safety area worth checking, especially in children.

---

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The mechanistic rationale is plausible, but the evidence is class-level and indirect. The trials test the propionate salt, not the furoate, and no furoate-specific data exist for atopic eczema. The marketed furoate products are inhaled or nasal, not topical, and the package insert safety data has not been obtained.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Package insert warnings and contraindications (blocking gap for safety screening)
- Mechanism of action data from DrugBank
- Confirmation of whether a topical fluticasone furoate formulation exists or is feasible
- Furoate-specific efficacy and safety data in atopic dermatitis, including HPA-axis and pediatric safety
- Confirmation of the original approved indication, to check overlap with existing labels
- Merge the duplicate "atopic eczema" and "dermatitis, atopic" predictions (rank 1 and rank 4 share identical evidence) to avoid double counting

**Other predictions in the pack:**
- **Bronchitis (L3):** the data are mostly COPD and asthma studies, and this overlaps existing marketed use, so it is not a clear repurposing case.
- **Contact dermatitis (L4):** no fluticasone furoate trials or literature were found.
- **2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate sensitization, occupational dermatitis and phototoxic dermatitis (L5):** model prediction only, with no trials or literature.
- **Asthma-related traits, susceptibility to (L5):** a genetic trait, not a treatable indication.
