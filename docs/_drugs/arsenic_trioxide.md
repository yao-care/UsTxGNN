# Arsenic Trioxide: From Acute Promyelocytic Leukemia to Myelodysplastic Syndrome

## One-Sentence Summary

Arsenic trioxide was originally used to treat **acute promyelocytic leukemia** and related myeloid leukemias.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **Myelodysplastic Syndrome (MDS)**,
with **19 clinical trials** and **20 publications** currently supporting this direction. These are mostly small, early-phase studies, and no Phase 3 RCT has isolated the drug's contribution in MDS.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism-of-action data for arsenic trioxide is not available in the data compiled for this report. Based on the linked literature, the drug acts through pro-apoptotic, antiproliferative, differentiation-promoting and anti-angiogenic effects. It is established in acute promyelocytic leukemia, where it is used with all-trans retinoic acid.

In MDS, laboratory and ex-vivo studies show arsenic trioxide induces apoptosis of MDS cells through NF-κB/FLIP signalling and modulation of BCL2-family genes. It also has epigenetic and differentiation effects when combined with decitabine and ascorbic acid. APL and MDS are both clonal myeloid disorders, and MDS can progress to acute myeloid leukemia, so extending an antileukemic agent to MDS is mechanistically plausible.

Clinical activity is modest. One review notes it is effective in roughly 20% of MDS patients. Arsenic trioxide is not approved for MDS.

- **Disease-level contraindication signals (from a knowledge graph, not label text):** long QT syndrome (including familial), torsades de pointes, atrioventricular block, kidney disease, liver disease, gallbladder disease, potassium deficiency, and pyoureter.
- **Drug Interactions:** 100 interactions were identified. Major-level examples include:

  - Cisapride
  - Clarithromycin

  - Granisetron

  Many of these are QT-prolonging or electrolyte-depleting agents. Moderate-level examples include famotidine, loperamide and several laxatives (bisacodyl, castor oil, glycerin, lactitol, lactulose, magnesium salts), likely reflecting electrolyte effects.
- **Guardrails noted in the MDS assessment:** QT prolongation, differentiation syndrome and hepatotoxicity.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Proceed with Guardrails**

**Rationale:**
Several completed Phase 1/2 and Phase 2 trials, a 2023 systematic review and a plausible apoptosis- and differentiation-based mechanism support arsenic trioxide in MDS. However, most studies are small or terminated, results are inconsistent, and no Phase 3 RCT isolates its effect. This supports further study under strict safety controls rather than an unconditional Go.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Confirmatory randomized data isolating arsenic trioxide's contribution in MDS, ideally in defined subgroups (for example low-risk MDS after ESA failure, or TP53-mutated disease, as in the recruiting trials)
- Results from the ongoing oral arsenic trials (NCT06778187, NCT06670222)
- Package insert warnings and contraindications, which are currently missing
- Mechanism-of-action data from DrugBank
- An ECG/QTc and electrolyte monitoring plan, with differentiation-syndrome and liver-function surveillance
- Review of the US regulatory pathway, since all listed authorizations are cancelled and the drug is not approved for MDS
- Lower-priority alternatives: aplastic anemia (L3, small non-randomized studies including a 100% non-response report) and Ewing sarcoma (L3, mainly preclinical) remain research questions. The remaining predictions have no supporting evidence and should be held.
